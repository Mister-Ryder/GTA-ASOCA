Abstract
Segmentation of tree-like structures in volumetric medical images presents unique1
challenges due to intrinsic anatomical heterogeneity—systematic variations in ap-2
pearance, structure, and connectivity across hierarchical levels. Pulmonary airways3
exemplify this "anatomical heterogeneity continuum" with: (1) intensity hetero-4
geneity, where voxel distributions vary significantly across generations of branches;5
and (2) structural heterogeneity, where morphology transitions from simple tubular6
structures to complex bifurcations. Conventional networks apply uniform repre-7
sentation strategies across all regions, fundamentally limiting their capacity for8
differentiated representation and creating an inherent contradiction between spatial9
accuracy and structural integrity, particularly the ability to maintain peripheral10
connectivity while preserving global precision. We introduce Heterogeneity-aware11
Adaptive Representation Learning (HARL), a novel framework that dynamically12
adjusts representation strategies according to region-specific anatomical character-13
istics. HARL comprises three key innovations: (1) an anatomical heterogeneity14
disentanglement encoder that explicitly models generation type, morphological15
structure, and spatial location attributes; (2) hierarchical adaptive modules that16
conditionally transform feature spaces based on anatomical attributes at different17
network levels; and (3) structure-preserving distance field regularization that implic-18
itly enforces topological consistency. Extensive experiments on three pulmonary19
CT datasets demonstrate that HARL not only significantly outperforms state-of-the-20
art methods, but also achieves remarkably consistent performance with standard21
deviations of merely 3.31% compared to conventional approaches reaching up to22
19.32% for structural metrics.Methodologically, it introduces a paradigm shift by23
explicitly modeling anatomical heterogeneity—applicable to vascular networks,24
neuronal pathways, and lymphatic systems.25

As illustrated in Figure 1, this heterogeneity manifests in two critical dimensions: (1) intensity36
heterogeneity, where voxel distributions vary dramatically from the trachea (diameter ∼20mm,37
clear contrast) to peripheral bronchioles; and (2) structural heterogeneity, where morphology tran-38
sitions from simple tubular structures to complex bifurcations with varying branching angles and39
thickness-to-lumen ratios. Our dataset analysis reveals significant non-uniform distributions across40
heterogeneity dimensions (see Figure 1, center), highlighting the challenge’s pervasiveness across41
different clinical conditions.42
These variations create fundamentally different representation requirements across regions—central43
airways demand precise boundary delineation, while peripheral branches prioritize connectivity44
preservation. As shown in the right panel of Figure 1, conventional segmentation approaches face45
an inherent trade-off: methods emphasizing boundary accuracy often produce terminal airway46
disconnections, while those prioritizing connectivity result in imprecise boundaries.47
Conventional deep learning approaches [5–8] employ uniform feature extraction and classification48
operations across the entire volume, fundamentally limiting their ability to address heterogeneous49
requirements [9–12]. This limitation creates what we term the representation allocation paradox:50
networks must either allocate representation capacity according to voxel prevalence (favoring high-51
volume central airways) or artificially balance them (potentially compromising precision in critical52
regions). Our analysis reveals this paradox manifests consistently across methodological categories:53
Generic volumetric networks (e.g., nnU-Net, 3D UX-Net) achieve high Dice scores but frequently fail54
to preserve peripheral airway connectivity, with significantly reduced performance in distal branches;55
Topology-constrained methods better maintain connectivity but sacrifice boundary precision and often56
require computationally expensive post-processing; Multi-task learning approaches incorporating57
anatomical knowledge show moderate improvements but still employ uniform feature extraction58
operations across all regions.59
We introduce Heterogeneity-aware Adaptive Representation Learning (HARL), a framework designed60
to fundamentally address the anatomical heterogeneity challenge. Rather than applying uniform61
representation strategies, HARL dynamically adapts its representation approach based on region-62
specific anatomical characteristics. This paradigm shift enables simultaneous optimization for63
different objectives—maintaining boundary precision in central airways while preserving connectivity64
in peripheral branches.65
HARL’s core innovation lies in its dynamic allocation of representation capacity through three66
integrated components: (1) Anatomical Heterogeneity Disentanglement, an encoder network that67
explicitly classifies voxels according to generation type, morphological structure, and spatial loca-68
tion; (2) Hierarchical Adaptive Modules that conditionally transform features based on anatomical69
attributes at strategic network levels; and (3) Structure-Preserving Distance Field Regularizationthat70
implicitly enforces topological consistency through Gaussian field reconstruction.71
2
Through extensive experiments on multiple datasets, we demonstrate that HARL significantly out-72
performs state-of-the-art methods, achieving both superior structural preservation and remarkably73

• Identification and formal characterization of the anatomical heterogeneity continuum in75
pulmonary airways and its impact on representation learning76
• A novel framework that dynamically adapts representation strategies according to region-77

• Three technical innovations: anatomical heterogeneity disentanglement, hierarchical adap-79

2 Related Work83
2.1 Dense Volumetric Segmentation in Medical Imaging84
V olumetric segmentation[5, 6] is dominated by encoder-decoder architectures like 3D U-Net [8] and85
V-Net [13], with subsequent refinements through nnU-Net [7] and architectural innovations [4, 11, 14].86
Despite their advancements, these approaches share a fundamental limitation: uniform convolutional87
operations that cannot adapt to regional anatomical variations, creating an inherent trade-off between88
spatial accuracy and structural preservation—the "representation allocation paradox." Attention89
mechanisms [15–17] and multi-scale techniques [18, 12, 19, 20] partially address this challenge but90
still apply identical transformation operations across anatomically distinct regions, failing to capture91
the full heterogeneity continuum.92
2.2 Anatomical Knowledge Integration and Topology Preservation93
Existing methods incorporate anatomical knowledge through topological constraints [ 4, 21, 22],94
multi-task learning with branch classification [23] or generation-type prediction [18], and structure-95
specific solutions [24–26]. While these approaches improve performance for specific metrics, they96
collectively suffer from treating anatomical attributes as auxiliary information rather than fundamental97
drivers of representation adaptation. They typically apply uniform operations across heterogeneous98
regions—either causing over-smoothing in central airways or insufficient preservation in peripheral99
branches—and fail to leverage anatomical characteristics to dynamically adapt strategies across the100
heterogeneity continuum. This heterogeneous performance manifests as large standard deviations in101
experimental results across test cases, empirically confirming the significant disparity in representation102
capability when confronting diverse anatomical regions along the heterogeneity continuum. HARL103
addresses these limitations by enabling anatomical attributes to directly modulate both feature104
extraction and processing, achieving region-specific optimization throughout the airway tree.105

3.1 Anatomical Heterogeneity Disentanglement119
We formalize airway heterogeneity along three complementary dimensions: Generation Type120
(G): Encodes the hierarchical position within the bronchial tree, from the trachea (generation 0)121
to peripheral bronchioles (generations 5-6). Morphological Structure (S): Characterizes local122
geometric configuration, distinguishing between simple tubular structures (classes 1-2), bifurcations123
(classes 3-4), and complex junctions (classes 5-6). Spatial Location (L): Maps the 3D anatomical124
position within the pulmonary system, differentiating between upper, middle, and lower lung regions,125
as well as left versus right lung placement, and trachea. Together, these attributes form a 3D attribute126
space:A(X) = {G(X),S (X),L (X)}, that comprehensively characterizes regional anatomical127
context.128
The Anatomical Heterogeneity Disentanglement (AHD) encoder explicitly models these attributes129
through a specialized multi-task classification network. The AHD encoder employs a shared backbone130
with channel attention mechanisms to capture multi-scale features:FA =EA(X), whereEA extracts131
rich anatomical features FA from the input volume. These features are processed by attribute-specific132
classification heads:133
G(X) =σG(WG· Pool(FA))∈ R7,S (X) = σS(WS· Pool(FA))∈ R7
L(X) =σL(WL· Pool(FA))∈ R7 (2)
The encoder incorporates channel-attention mechanisms through Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) blocks134
and multi-scale feature aggregation to capture both fine-grained details and broader contextual135
information. Additionally, the encoder generates a spatially-aware anatomical guidance map: GA =136

peripheral airways and certain morphological structures are significantly underrepresented. We138
employ a focal loss variant:139
LAHD =λGLFL(G(X), YG) +λSLFL(S(X), YS) +λLLFL(L(X), YL) (3)
with class-specific weighting factors that emphasize underrepresented classes (particularly peripheral140

explicit disentanglement approach provides several advantages: interpretability of anatomical context,142
targeted adaptation for region-specific challenges, improved data efficiency through structured143
inductive bias, and a generalizable framework for characterizing anatomical heterogeneity.144

4 Experiment221
4.1 Datasets and Setup222
We evaluated HARL on three pulmonary CT datasets: BAS [27] and AeroPath [28]. BAS includes223
90 cases (20 from EXACT’09, 70 from LIDC) with varied clinical conditions. AeroPath contains224
27 CT scans from patients with pathologies ranging from emphysema to large tumors, providing a225
challenging test for generalization capabilities. For BAS dataset experimentation, we adopted the226
standardized evaluation protocol established in previous benchmarks to ensure fair comparison with227
6
existing methods. This includes the official train/test splits and preprocessing pipelines consistent228
with community practices. Preprocessing involved HU thresholding [-1000, 600] and comprehensive229
augmentation techniques (random flipping, affine transformations, noise addition). Our network230
architecture extends the 3D U-Net [ 8] with residual connections, integrating our proposed AHD231
encoder and hierarchical adaptive modules.232
Our evaluation metrics comprehensively capture both spatial accuracy (DSC, Sensitivity, Precision)233
and structural integrity (Branch Detection, Tree-length Detection). This dual evaluation approach234
directly addresses our central thesis: heterogeneity-aware representation enables simultaneous opti-235
mization of boundary precision and connectivity preservation across the anatomical heterogeneity236
continuum. To comprehensively evaluate segmentation performance across both spatial accuracy and237
structural preservation dimensions, we compute an overall SCORE metric following the weighted238
formula: SCORE = 0.3×TL + 0.3×BD + 0.2×DIC + 0.1×SEN + 0.1×PRE. This weighting scheme,239
inspired by the ATM22 competition framework, appropriately emphasizes structural integrity metrics240
(TL, BD) while still accounting for volumetric accuracy (DIC, SEN, PRE), aligning with clinical241
priorities for airway analysis.242

5 Conclusion322
We introduced HARL (Heterogeneity-aware Adaptive Representation Learning), a novel framework323
for dense volumetric segmentation of pulmonary airways that addresses anatomical heterogene-324
ity. HARL dynamically adapts representation strategies according to region-specific characteristics325
through: (1) an anatomical heterogeneity disentanglement encoder modeling generation type, mor-326
phology, and location; (2) hierarchical adaptive modules transforming features based on anatomical327
attributes; and (3) structure-preserving distance field regularization enforcing topological consistency.328
Experiments across multiple datasets show HARL outperforms state-of-the-art methods by: achieving329
superior structural preservation in airways (94.85% tree-length detection), exhibiting lower variance330
across metrics (±3.31% vs. ±7.93%), and successfully balancing spatial accuracy with structural in-331
tegrity—resolving the "representation allocation paradox" facing conventional uniform-representation332
approaches.333
5.1 Limitations334
Limitations include: dependence on quality annotations for attribute extraction, higher computational335
complexity than conventional networks, and tendency toward over-segmentation despite improved336
connectivity. The theoretical framework, while demonstrated effectively on airways, requires further337
validation for other tree-like anatomical structures.338
5.2 Broader Impact339
Clinically, HARL enables earlier detection of small airway diseases through complete peripheral340
airway extraction. Methodologically, it introduces a paradigm shift by explicitly modeling anatomical341
heterogeneity—applicable to vascular networks, neuronal pathways, and lymphatic systems. The342
integration of explicit anatomical knowledge into deep learning demonstrates a path toward more343
interpretable medical AI systems that dynamically adjust processing strategies based on region-344
specific characteristics, potentially increasing clinical trust and adoption.345
